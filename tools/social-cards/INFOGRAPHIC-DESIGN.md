# What makes an infographic a showstopper (researched 01/11/2026)

Kristian, 01/11/2026: "more with like lines between them and like harnesses ... the MCP in the
middle, what it is, the integrations ... research infographics online to see what the best ones
are ... educational, exciting, cool to look at, and is a showstopper."

## What the evidence says

- **A diagram beats a list.** The technical visuals that travel on LinkedIn are system diagrams:
  ByteByteGo's architecture drawings (labelled boxes, arrows that say what flows, dashed boxes for
  groups) and the MCP architecture posts (Hosts -> Clients -> Servers -> Tools). Our own bar, "The
  self-improving harness", is the same thing: numbered stages joined by arrows and a loop back.
- **Context makes a number land.** A 2026 conjoint study of infographic preferences ("What Catches
  the Eye?", arXiv 2605.27554) found the comparison shown drove 58.5% of preference: a percentage
  drawn against a 0-50-100% scale raised choice by 34.6 points, and a number with no scale or
  benchmark lowered it by 41.6. Icon series and bar charts beat a single icon (+8.3 and +6.1 against
  -14.4). Colour (red vs blue) made no practical difference.
- **One main point, a clear reading path.** Every guide agrees (Ceros 2026, Venngage, the PMC
  review "Designing Infographics", Traboco et al.): one sentence the reader can repeat, numbered
  stages, white space, 3-4 colours, 2 typefaces, sources on the image. Infographics increase
  attention and recall against text (Kunze 2021; Hughes 2021; Huang 2018 RCT).
- **Our own numbers agree.** Our best performers were diagrams or tables that teach: MCP "Wrap the
  job" (296), the Eventbrite fee table (299), "Copying software was never the hard part" (242).

## The rules for every image from infographic.py

1. Prefer a diagram. Use `hub` (your AI -> MCP in the middle -> your tools, with labelled wires,
   dashed zones and a gate strip) or `flow` (numbered stages with arrows) whenever the lesson has
   parts that connect. Use `bars` for percentages, never a bare percentage. `versus`, `rules`,
   `steps` and `stats` are for lessons with no moving parts.
2. Label the wires with verbs (asks, acts, waits, reports, decides). One label per side is enough.
3. Number the zones (01, 02, 03) in reading order. Left to right, top to bottom.
4. Every node gets an icon; a dashed node means "not yet" or "outside our reach".
5. One headline ending in the brand's coloured full stop; one line of sub; source in mono.
6. Look at the render at full size before it ships: no crossing labels, no wrapped chips, no
   empty half page.
